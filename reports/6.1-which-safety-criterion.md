# Roadmap 6.1: Which safety criterion SDR uses

Date: 2026-11-20

## The short answer

The long gap behind trucks was the only safety rule that cost a lot of capacity. This item asked whether SDR needs it. That depends on what "safe enough" means, so SDR and today's road were compared hazard by hazard, counted the same way.

Without the gap, and with every truck enclosed as the SDR-Ready standard now requires, SDR is still below today's road on every hazard compared:
- **Lost loads:** half as many collisions per lost load (2.4 against 5.0) and no pile-ups of five or more vehicles.
- **Sudden stops:** about a quarter as many collisions per stop.
- **Objects lying ahead and breakdowns:** none on SDR.
- **Over a billion kilometres:** the risk model puts SDR at about 9 object collisions per billion vehicle-km against about 25 for today's road. That holds in every combination of its inputs.

On that evidence you chose the criterion **"clearly safer than today overall, and no worse than today for any single kind of hazard"**, and D1 drops the gap. SDR then carries about 31 % more than the simulated today's road at 10 % trucks, instead of 11 %.

On the way, two flaws in the risk model came to light, and both had made SDR look better than it is. Its best figure rises from about 3 to about 7 collisions per billion vehicle-km.

## 6.1 Which safety criterion SDR uses

**The question.** Until now SDR read "safety first" as "safe against each foreseeable event". That reading is what demanded a brick-wall gap behind every truck, the one rule that costs much capacity: SDR carried +11 % at 10 % trucks with it and +31 % without it. Today's drivers keep no such gap. A different reading was possible: clearly safer than today overall, and no worse than today for any single kind of hazard. Whether SDR without the gap meets that was unknown. The two roads had been counted differently, and the risk model had no row for relative separation with fast sensing.

**What was done.**
- **A new experiment group** puts each hazard on today's road and on SDR, counted the same way. The hazards are a lost load that stops dead, one that slides, a sudden ("brick-wall") stop, an object lying 150 m ahead, and a breakdown. Each row has 30 runs on the same traffic, at 4,200 + 600 veh/h with 10 % trucks, and records the collisions the hazard caused and their impact speeds.
- **Three versions of SDR:** without the truck gap, with it, and without it but with the truck reporting its own lost load at once.
- **The risk model gained:**
  - today's road on the same measure;
  - relative separation with fast sensing;
  - the SDR-Ready fleet, with and without the truck gap;
  - a split by where an object comes from (lying in the lane, truck cargo, truck tyres and parts, cars and vans);
  - for every hazard, the ratio of SDR to today's road over every combination of its inputs.
- **Two new inputs for the SDR-Ready fleet,** both assumptions:
  - how much cargo an enclosed body still lets out (10 %, range 2–25 %);
  - how many tyre and wheel losses are reported beforehand as a fault (30 %, range 0–60 %). Such a truck then gets the long gap.

**What came out.** Per event, from 30 runs per row on the same traffic:

| Hazard | Today's road | SDR without the truck gap | SDR with the truck gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lost load that stops dead: collisions per event | 5.0 | **2.4** | 0 |
| — impacts above 30 km/h per event; worst impact | 4.5; 92 km/h | 1.8; 79 km/h | 0 |
| — events with 5 or more vehicles involved | 11 of 30 | 0 | 0 |
| Lost load that slides | 0.13 | 0 | 0 |
| Brick-wall stop: collisions per event | 3.4 | **0.9** | 0.5 |
| Object 150 m ahead | 0.17 | 0 | 0 |
| Breakdown | 0 | 0 | 0 |

- **These differences are outside the noise:**
  - SDR against today's road, for the lost load that stops dead and the brick-wall stop;
  - the truck gap against no gap, for the lost load and, just, for the brick-wall stop. A truck that stops dead has a long gap behind it.
- **Too rare to tell the roads apart in 30 runs:** the sliding load and the object ahead.
- **A truck that reports its own loss at once did no better** (2.43 against 2.40 per event). The follower is about 19 m behind and cannot stop, whoever knows.

Per billion vehicle-km (risk model, central value, with the range when the researched inputs vary):

| Setup | Object collisions |
|---|---|
| Today's road | 25 (21–75) |
| SDR without the truck gap, SDR-Ready fleet (now recommended) | **9.2 (6.7–30)** |
| The same with the truck gap | 7.3 (5.2–25) |
| Hybrid with the truck gap, today's vehicles (formerly "about 5", and "about 3" with load monitoring) | 7.2 (5.2–25) |

- **Per hazard, SDR ÷ today's road is below 1 in every combination of inputs.** Objects lying in the lane 0.38; truck cargo 0.05; truck tyres and parts 0.34; losses from cars and vans 0.48; all objects 0.37 (range 0.28–0.44).
- **The fleet is not the whole story.** Against a today's road whose trucks were built the same way, SDR still has about half the collisions.
- **Cars and vans, not trucks, are now the biggest residual.** About 7 of SDR's 9 collisions come from what falls off them, and keeping the truck gap would remove only about 2.

**Two corrections to the risk model.** Both had flattered SDR.
1. **Lost loads were counted at the brick-wall rate.** A lost load at a relative gap counted as many collisions as a brick-wall stop (1.5). The simulator measures 2.4, so the hybrid with fast sensing rises from about 5 to about 7.
2. **A reported loss was counted as harmless.** A loss the vehicle reports itself counted as harmless whatever the gap. At a relative gap it is not, as above. "Vehicles reporting their own lost cargo" therefore no longer lowers the figure: that row goes from about 3 to about 7. Load monitoring is worth something only where the follower has room to use the warning, or where it keeps the load in.

**Finish line.**
- *SDR-01 states which safety criterion SDR uses (the user's decision):* **met.** You chose it, and SDR-01 §2 states it with what it means in practice. D1 and D10 follow from it, and §10 notes what it asks of liability and the public.
- *SDR-04 shows per hazard whether relative separation with SDR-grade sensing meets "no worse than today", next to its capacity:* **met.** SDR-04 §2 has the per-hazard table and the per-hazard risk ratios. The verdict and §1 give the capacity without the gap: +31 % / +16 % / +3 % at 10 / 20 / 30 % trucks, and about +12 to +26 % against a real merge.
- *Risk-model rows for relative separation with SDR-grade sensing and the SDR-Ready fleet, with the residual from tyres and parts given separately and what monitoring takes off it:* **met.** Tyres and parts give 1.6 collisions per billion vehicle-km with 30 % reported beforehand, and 2.3 with none.
- *The lost-load figures counted the same way first:* **met.** Both roads are counted as collisions caused per event, from the same seeds and observation window.

## What it means together

- **D1 changes.** SDR keeps a long gap behind vehicles that report a fault (a tyre or wheel warning included) and where sensing is degraded, not behind every truck.
- **The capacity case is much stronger.** It is about +31 % on the simulated corridor at 10 % trucks instead of +11 %, and it no longer collapses on freight routes (+16 % at 20 % trucks, +3 % at 30 %). Against a real merge the gain is no longer "somewhere between +5 % and −7 %" but about +12 to +26 %.
- **The safety case rests on different things now:**
  - SDR has half as many collisions per lost load as today's road, because its followers are closer and brake at once, so they hit slower and fewer pile up;
  - SDR-Ready trucks shed less;
  - fast roadside sensing, as before.
- **The risk model now treats load monitoring differently.** Reporting a loss as it happens is worth nothing at a relative gap. What is worth something is a body that keeps the load in, and a tyre or wheel fault reported before anything comes off.
- **Documents changed:**
  - SDR-01: §2 (the criterion), §4, §6, §10, D1, D9 and D10;
  - SDR-03: §6 (risk model) and the D1 proposal;
  - SDR-04: the fourth revision note, the verdict, §1, §2, §3, §4, §5 and the appendix;
  - README: where things stand;
  - ROADMAP: 1.5 and 1.6 are overtaken by this item, and 6.3 and 6.4 are new.

## Limits and open questions

- **Today's road may be counted too harshly.** Its simulated drivers brake but never swerve (roadmap 2.9), so it may count too many collisions per lost load. SDR keeps its advantage on lost loads unless today's road really has 63 % fewer than simulated, or 52 % fewer for losses from cars and vans. That margin is wide, but it rests on a driver model that is known to overdo pile-ups. When 2.9 adds swerving, this comparison should be the first thing re-run.
- **The fleet figures are assumptions.** Nobody measures how often an enclosed truck still sheds something that stops a car, or how early tyre and wheel monitoring warns (roadmap 4.4). Across their ranges the verdict holds, but SDR's figure moves between about 8.5 and 9.9.
- **The level of every figure rests on two inputs.** These are the share of objects heavy enough to stop a car dead and the object rate: a factor of 4 and 3.5. They move both roads alike, so the comparison holds, but the absolute numbers are orders of magnitude.
- **The model's today's road is not calibrated.** It sits above the recorded debris-crash rate (about 25 against about 10). That is expected, since a crash can hold several collisions and most debris does not stop a car, but it is still not a calibration.
- **The simulator still runs the old hybrid by default,** with the truck gap. Weather, radio, layouts and sensitivity have not been re-run with the new D1. That is proposed as item 6.4, together with the ice policy, which was chosen with the gap in place.
- **Losses from cars and vans are now the largest residual,** and no rule covers them. Proposed as item 6.3.
- **Everything here was run at 10 % trucks, dry, at moderate demand.**
- **Not done:** the public and liability side is only noted (SDR-01 §10). A machine-run road will probably be judged more strictly than human drivers, and the operator chose the gap where a driver did not.
